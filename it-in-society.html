IT in Society

Technology now touches almost every part of daily life. This page covers how money has gone digital, how organisations mine data for patterns, what social networks do to the people who use them, how IT has changed different sectors, and how technology is used to teach and learn.

Digital currencies 12.1

Electronic currency is a payment that happens in digital form, without the exchange of physical currency such as coins and bank notes. There are several types of electronic currency:

  • Digital currency
  • Virtual currency
  • Cryptocurrency
  • Central bank digital base money
  • Stored value cards

Digital currency

Digital currency is money that is available in a digital or electronic form. Currency is exchanged digitally between computers, instantly, rather than by handing over physical bank notes and coins. Popular ways to pay are credit cards, mobile phones and smart watches, and it makes transactions across borders easy.

CharacteristicsUses
Exists only in electronic form, with no coins or notesPaying in shops by card, phone or smart watch
Moves instantly between computersBuying goods and services online
Can be sent across borders and converted between currenciesTransferring money between accounts, including internationally
Can be centralised or decentralisedPaying bills and making in-app purchases

Digital currencies can be centralised — there is a central body that manages and regulates the use of the currency — or decentralised — there is no central body managing the process. The most common way to manage transactions in a decentralised system is with a ledger, for example a blockchain.

Centralised — every payment passes through one central body, such as a bank

Centralised systems

  • Credit/debit card — used with card-reading machines. The card can be swiped (the magnetic strip is read), inserted (the chip is read) or tapped (contactless — touching the card to the machine is all that is needed). In every case the data is read, the bank is located from the data, and a request is sent to deduct the amount from the customer's balance and transfer it to the business's bank account.
  • Electronic point of sale (EPOS) — the computerised system used in shops to enter sales information, run customer loyalty programmes, manage stock and so on. It is the checkout point where the customer's transaction is made electronically. (A traditional POS may involve only physical cash, with records kept in register books.)

Decentralised systems

  • Bitcoin — a form of cryptocurrency that aims to remove the need for central authorities such as banks or governments. It uses blockchain technology to support peer-to-peer transactions between users on a decentralised network.
  • Litecoin — a cryptocurrency created from a fork of the Bitcoin blockchain to complete transactions quickly and cheaply. It is also peer-to-peer and decentralised.
  • Peer-to-peer electronic monetary systems — payment systems designed so that peers can send electronic payments to each other without going through a central body such as a financial institution.

Mobile electronic wallet

  • A method of contactless payment made with a mobile phone, or a smart watch connected to a phone.
  • The user downloads an app that stores their credit card information and other required details.
  • The device is then used as a digital credit/debit card, or electronic wallet.
  • It allows payment for goods and services digitally.
  • Data is read from the device and a request is sent to the bank to transfer money from the user's bank account.

Setting up a mobile wallet — this process can be asked as a 4-mark question:

  1. The user downloads the app from a finance provider.
  2. The user registers with the provider in the app.
  3. The user enters their phone number to receive a verification code by SMS.
  4. The user authenticates the number by entering the verification code.
  5. The user enters credit card details or adds account details, which links the wallet to their bank account.
  6. The user authenticates the payment details.

Advantages and disadvantages of digital currency

Advantages

  • No need to carry physical money around
  • Provides fixed records of transactions
  • If a card, phone or watch is stolen or lost, the bank can be contacted to block all monetary transactions
  • Contactless payment can speed up the payment process
  • International payments can be made easily from one currency to another
  • Contactless payments don't need a PIN each time
  • Daily or monthly transaction limits can be set, which helps prevent overspending

Disadvantages

  • There is often a limit on contactless payments
  • Data sent electronically can be hacked
  • There is a risk of being charged for someone else's transaction if you walk past a machine that accepts contactless payment
  • Money can be stolen through contactless payment while you are within the range that lets transactions go through

Virtual currency

Virtual currency is a decentralised system. It exists only within a virtual environment and has no physical form such as bank notes and coins. An example is tokens that can be collected or bought in computer games, letting the user buy new powers or clothing. For this reason a virtual currency is normally specific to the game or application it was created for, and cannot be used elsewhere. Another example is on streaming platforms, where in-app tokens can be bought to trigger special effects or messages that are displayed in real time on the video stream.

Advantages

  • Personalises a game or application by making it fun to collect and spend its own currency
  • No expensive manufacturing or physical storage costs
  • Can be programmed to complete automated transactions
  • Increases transaction speeds and removes geographical boundaries

Disadvantages

  • It is a decentralised system, so has little regulation
  • Usually specific to the context it was created for and can't be spent elsewhere
  • Can be subject to scams

Cryptocurrency

Cryptocurrency is a digital or virtual currency secured by cryptography, which makes it very difficult to counterfeit or to spend twice (double spend). Many cryptocurrencies are decentralised networks based on blockchain technology — a distributed ledger enforced by a network of separate computers. They are a type of peer-to-peer electronic monetary system. A key feature is that they are generally not issued by any central authority, which makes them resistant to government interference or manipulation. Bitcoin and Litecoin are well-known examples.

Advantages

  • Minimal fund-transfer fees compared with banks
  • Difficult to counterfeit
  • Potential for high returns, so it can be attractive as an investment

Disadvantages

  • The decentralised system allows a level of anonymity, which can encourage criminal activity that goes untraced, such as money laundering and tax evasion
  • High volatility and potential for large losses
  • Because it is unregulated and not backed by a central authority, it is open to cyber attacks and hacking

Central bank digital base money

Central bank digital base money, also called central bank digital currency (CBDC), is the digital form of fiat money. Fiat money is the physical money used in a country that is issued by its state bank for paying for goods and services. Digital fiat money digitalises real-world money, effectively creating a digital currency. Like paper bank notes it has its own serial number, which makes it distinguishable and prevents counterfeiting. Banks intend to use it as a controlled supply alongside the existing currency.

Advantages

  • Helps prevent crimes such as money laundering and tax evasion, because transactions can be traced
  • Cost of producing money falls, because no paper or printing is needed
  • Banks can exchange money much faster
  • Simplifies the implementation of monetary policy and government functions

Disadvantages

  • Users may have to give up some privacy
  • As a digital currency it is vulnerable to cyber attacks that could lead to loss of funds or sensitive information

Stored value cards

Stored value cards are similar to credit cards, but have a set amount of currency stored on them. The card can be used to pay for items until the stored value reaches 0. There are two types:

TypeHow it worksExample
Closed loopA monetary value can be loaded onto the card only once, and it is discarded when all the money is spentGift cards bought for particular stores
Open loopA monetary value can be loaded onto the card repeatedlyCollege cafeteria cards that can be recharged many times

Advantages

  • Prevents overspending and debt, because only the set amount can be used unless recharged
  • Some stores don't carry out a credit check or minimum-balance check, which makes the cards easy to access
  • Payments don't affect the user's credit history, which keeps a level of anonymity

Disadvantages

  • Some have an inactivity fee or expiry — after a period of no use the stored value expires or a fee is charged
  • Purchases don't help build up credit, so some people prefer not to use them
  • Closed loop cards limit purchasing power
  • There can be fees to reload a card, sometimes making it cheaper to pay with a normal credit card or cash

Impacts and risks of digital currencies

Cryptocurrency in particular is often discussed for its disadvantages, but the impact differs depending on who you are:

Who is affectedImpact and risks
BanksLose fee income when payments bypass them, for example cryptocurrency transfers between peers. They may respond by issuing digital currency of their own.
Governments and tax authoritiesAnonymity lets money laundering and tax evasion go untraced, and decentralised currencies are hard to regulate and can weaken a government's control of its money supply. CBDC transactions can be traced, which helps prevent this.
BusinessesLower transfer fees, faster international payments and access to customers who prefer to pay digitally; but volatile values, the cost of new systems, and the risk of scams or hacked payment data.
IndividualsConvenient and no cash to carry, with spending limits available; but data can be hacked, prices can swing and cause large losses, a lost or stolen device can mean lost money, and CBDC may mean less privacy.
SocietyPeople without a smartphone, internet access or a bank account can be left out (the digital divide). Mining some cryptocurrencies uses large amounts of electricity.

Data mining 12.2

Data mining is a form of data analysis and artificial intelligence. It is the process of collecting large sets of data and analysing them to identify patterns and trends. This information can identify past and future trends that are very valuable to a company. The large sets of data are stored in data warehouses. Computers are used for data mining because of the sheer volume of data that has to be searched and analysed.

The six stages of data mining

The stages run in order, and results from later stages can send you back to earlier ones

StageWhat it meansExample
1. Business understandingWork out what the business wants to achieve with the data, and why the data is being examinedA store wants to increase sales, so the goal is finding which products are popular with customers
2. Data understandingGet to know the data you have: what information is available and what it looks likeFor customer behaviour, look at purchase history, demographics and preferences
3. Data preparationClean up and organise the data so it is ready for analysis: fix errors, handle missing information, get it into a useful formatIf some customer records are incomplete, fill in the missing details or remove the incomplete entries
4. Data modellingBuild models that can find patterns or make predictions, choosing the right techniquesUse algorithms to predict which products customers might want, based on their past purchases
5. EvaluationCheck how well the models perform: are they accurate, and do they meet the goals set in stage 1?Check whether the model's predictions match customers' actual buying behaviour
6. DeploymentPut the findings into use in the real world so the business benefits from the insightsIf the model suggests certain marketing strategies, use them in real marketing campaigns

Stage 1: Business understanding

  • Business goals and their impact are identified (the need of the business)
  • The situation is assessed to determine what needs to be achieved or solved to reach the goal (what is the problem?)
  • Goals for the data mining process are defined (the objectives that will overcome the problem)
  • A plan is made to develop a roadmap for the data mining process, outlining tasks and timelines to achieve the business objectives

Stage 2: Data understanding

  • The initial collection of data takes place, to explore what data is available
  • The data is documented
  • The sources of the data that has been gathered are listed
  • Data quality is assessed and verified to identify any issues that need to be addressed (checking completeness, accuracy and so on)
  • Variables are identified — recognising the relevant variables that may affect the analysis and decision making

Stage 3: Data preparation

  • Data selection — relevant data is chosen from the available resources
  • Data cleansing — errors are corrected, missing values are inserted, and incorrect or inconsistent data is removed
  • Data transformation — data is constructed and formatted so that it is in the form the business needs
  • This allows patterns and trends related to the business needs to be established in the data

Stage 4: Data modelling

  • Appropriate data mining algorithms are selected, based on the nature of the problem
  • Models are created by running the selected algorithms on existing (historical) data
  • Model parameters are adjusted to improve accuracy and performance
  • Models are validated by testing them on new data, to make sure they generalise well and give reliable results

Stage 5: Evaluation

  • Models are evaluated by assessing their performance and how well they meet the defined goals and objectives
  • Results are interpreted to understand the insights the models generate, and to identify existing patterns and look for new ones
  • Models are compared to find the most effective one
  • Iterate if necessary — if results are not satisfactory, adjustments are made to the models or the data preparation process

Stage 6: Deployment

  • Models are implemented in the business environment or systems for practical use
  • Performance is monitored and documented
  • Final results are reported to stakeholders
  • Results are reviewed to check for errors and to decide how to correct them

Uses of data mining

National security and surveillance — data mining analyses huge sets of data to find hidden patterns, predict potential threats and take proactive measures to keep civilians and governments safe and secure.

  • Identifying threats — finding patterns that suggest terrorist activity, cyber attacks or other criminal actions; for example, spotting threats of violence in social media comment sections
  • Monitoring communications — searching recorded phone calls, saved emails or other channels for keywords or patterns that suggest suspicious activity
  • Predictive analysis — mining previous criminal records from police stations to find trends and patterns that may help predict and prevent another security incident
  • Enhancing border security — analysing travel records and other data at borders to identify individuals who may pose a threat
  • Cyber security — protecting the computers and networks that hold confidential government data, by recognising patterns that indicate a cyber attack
  • Facial recognition and biometrics — mining data from CCTV footage or airport check-ins to identify and track individuals who pose a threat

Businesses — businesses use data mining to understand their customers and operations better, in order to increase sales and gain an advantage over competitors.

  • Predicting customers' needs — looking at buying habits helps companies understand their audience and create items and services that suit it
  • Running targeted marketing — customer profiles can be created so that customers are targeted with special offers and promotions they are more likely to be interested in
  • Predicting trends — past trends can be recognised and future ones predicted, so businesses can identify their best-selling item, restock it and create similar items
  • Improving sales strategies — identifying when, what and where customers buy the most, and adjusting inventory and sales schedules to the times that data mining predicts
  • Spotting fraud — identifying suspicious transactions and building a blocklist of credit card numbers, addresses or sellers, to prevent loss by refusing their orders
  • Enhancing customer service — analysing customer feedback and complaints to improve service

Scientific research — data mining helps researchers speed up their work by digging into big data, finding patterns, making predictions and coming up with new discoveries and solutions.

  • Healthcare research — doctors and scientists find patterns in medical data, which helps them predict diseases, understand how treatments work and improve patient care
  • Environmental studies — analysing data such as air quality or temperature to track climate change, predict natural disasters or find ways to protect endangered animals
  • Market research — analysing shopping patterns, online reviews and social media to understand what customers want, make better products and improve sales strategies
  • Educational research — educators and policymakers look at test scores, study habits and other data to understand how students learn and to improve teaching methods and materials
  • Genetic research — mining genetic data to learn about DNA, disease and health, which helps find new treatments and shows how genetics affect health
  • Astronomy — handling the vast amount of data from telescopes and space missions, which helps discover new stars and galaxies
  • Social science research — analysing data from surveys, social media and other sources to study human behaviour, social trends and cultural patterns

Healthcare — data mining helps healthcare providers make smarter decisions, predict diseases, personalise treatments and improve overall patient care.

  • Predicting diseases — finding patterns in patient records that predict illnesses such as heart disease or diabetes before they become serious
  • Personalised treatment — analysing a patient's health data to tailor treatment to them, which can be more effective than one-size-fits-all solutions
  • Drug development — pharmaceutical companies analyse research data, speeding up the discovery of new drugs and making development more efficient
  • Managing healthcare costs — hospitals and clinics look at treatment costs and patient outcomes to provide better care at lower cost
  • Reducing hospital readmissions — predicting which patients are likely to be readmitted, so providers can take preventive measures
  • Preventing fraud and abuse — detecting false insurance claims or prescription misuse by analysing patterns in billing and clinical data

Analysis of social and economic trends — data mining helps experts sift through mountains of data to spot patterns, predict changes and make better-informed decisions about the future.

  • Understanding consumer behaviour — examining shopping patterns, online searches and social media activity to reveal what people buy and why, and trends in preferences and spending
  • Predicting market trends — analysing financial data, stock markets and economic indicators to predict future trends, helping investors and businesses decide where to invest or which products might be popular
  • Analysing social media — looking at posts, likes and shares to identify trends in public opinion, popular culture and political movements
  • Tracking employment and economic growth — understanding job-market trends, unemployment rates and areas of growth or decline, which governments need for planning
  • Real estate analysis — analysing changes in property values and rental prices, helping both buyers and sellers make better decisions
  • Identifying public health trends — spotting the spread of diseases or the effectiveness of health campaigns
  • Assessing environmental impact — revealing trends in pollution levels or climate change, which is vital for planning and policy making

Ethical and privacy concerns

Data mining is very useful, but it also raises important ethical and privacy concerns:

  • Privacy invasion — it often involves personal data such as shopping habits, social media activity or medical records. People may not know their data is being used this way, which can feel like an invasion of privacy.
  • Data security — when data is collected and stored for mining, it could be stolen or misused. A hacker could access sensitive information such as bank details or private messages.
  • Misuse of information — the information can be used in ways that harm people, for example to deny someone a loan, a job or insurance based on their data profile.
  • Bias and discrimination — the algorithms may be biased and make unfair assumptions about people based on their age, gender, race or where they live, which can lead to discrimination.
  • Consent issues — people often don't explicitly agree (or don't know) that their data is being mined, which raises the question of whether it is ethical to use their data without clear permission.
  • Transparency — it can be hard to understand how data mining works and what is done with the data, which can make people distrustful.

Advantages and disadvantages of data mining

Advantages

  • Allows organisations to make strategic decisions that help maintain or increase their revenue
  • Allows organisations to understand their customers and create the products they need
  • Allows individuals to see targeted product advertising based on the things they already like

Disadvantages

  • Software tools and skilled staff are required
  • Many people see data mining as both unethical and an invasion of their privacy
  • Storage costs for the data are very high, which increases the cost of data mining
  • A great security issue, because hackers want access to data that has such high value
  • The outcomes are only predictions based on patterns and trends in past data. It is not an exact science, and predictions can be wrong

Social networking 12.3

Social networking is the use of websites and apps that allow users to communicate and interact with each other.

Chat rooms

A chat room is an online service that allows multiple users to type messages to each other, so they can chat. Some chat rooms are public and open to anyone; others are private and often need an invitation link or passcode to join. They don't need physical presence, so anyone can join from around the world through the internet. Some support emotes, links and images, but the conversation is mainly text. People use chat rooms to discuss subjects, exchange information or just socialise. They are often found on websites, forums or platforms where people with shared interests gather, and businesses may use them to test or review items, or for customer support chat. In a chat room, messages are visible to all members.

Instant messaging

Instant messaging is real-time communication that lets users exchange text messages instantly over the internet, one-to-one or in groups. It supports multimedia such as images, sound, video and emojis. Most instant messaging facilities have read receipts (showing who has read a message), an online status (showing who is online right now) and an option to turn on notifications of incoming messages. A friend or contact list is also common, letting users send a chat request or block or remove a contact. Many apps include encryption and privacy features to secure the content of messages.

Forums

A forum is an online platform or website where people hold discussions by posting messages — like a digital bulletin board. Users create topic threads on specific subjects and contribute to ongoing conversations. Each message can be a question, comment or reply, and threads hold a series of related messages in a structured way. Forums are used to share information, ask questions and connect with others who have similar interests or expertise, on topics from hobbies and technology to professional advice and support groups. Threads are organised into discussion groups often called sub-forums. Many forums have rules for posts and moderators who remove posts that break the rules or are off-topic. Some are membership-only, needing an account to post and sometimes even to view earlier threads. Because messages are usually archived, users can go back and refer to past discussions.

Email

Email (electronic mail) is a digital communication method that lets users send and receive messages over the internet. It works through a system of unique addresses for each user, so messages are delivered precisely. An email typically has a subject line, a main body containing the message, and the option to attach files or documents. Users have an inbox for incoming messages and a sent folder for the emails they have sent. Emails can be replied to or forwarded to others. Security measures such as spam filters and encryption protect users from unwanted content and improve privacy. Email serves personal and professional purposes, and is fast but asynchronous — the other person doesn't need to respond at the same moment.

Blogs

Blogs are online journals or informational websites where individuals or organisations regularly post articles, insights or stories. They are typically displayed in reverse chronological order, with the most recent content first. Writers share thoughts, experiences and expertise on topics from personal life to specialised subjects, often in an informal, conversational tone. Besides text, blogs can include images, videos and links to other websites. Readers can usually interact with the blogger through comments, which builds a community around the blog. Blogs began as personal online diaries and have grown to include professional and corporate blogs, and they are now important in digital marketing, journalism and personal expression.

Microblogs

Microblogs let users share brief, concise updates, typically as short text messages. Their defining feature is a character limit on each post, restricting messages to a few sentences or a limited number of characters. Users can share real-time information, thoughts and links quickly. Microblogging platforms prioritise immediate, frequent updates, so users keep up an ongoing conversation with their followers. They play a key role in spreading timely information, engaging audiences and encouraging concise communication.

Social media

Social media refers to online platforms and technologies that let users create, share and exchange information, ideas and content in virtual communities. They support interactive communication through text, images, videos and links, and let individuals, businesses and organisations connect with a global audience. Key features are user-generated content, connecting with friends or followers, real-time interaction and sharing multimedia content. Social media shapes public discourse, spreads information and builds online communities.

Impact of social networking on individuals

Intellectual isolation is when a person doesn't get to hear or learn about different ideas or opinions — like being in a bubble where you only see things one way, with little chance to learn from other viewpoints or experiences. It can happen because:

  • Algorithms show content based on user preferences, creating filter bubbles that limit exposure to diverse perspectives
  • Connecting with like-minded people creates echo chambers that limit different viewpoints
  • The ability to block, remove and like content gives only selective exposure
  • Joining online communities on forums and chat rooms easily can limit exposure to a broad range of ideas
  • Confirmation bias — users actively seek information that confirms their beliefs, reinforcing their own perspectives
  • Certain negative or stereotypical views are amplified, which reduces engagement with other views and adds to polarisation
  • Less face-to-face interaction limits the deeper conversation that takes place in real life and helps you explore new ideas
  • Fear of missing out (FOMO) and following the latest trends pressures users to agree with popular ideas online, limiting critical thinking

Physical health

  • Excessive use of social media leads to sedentary behaviour, reducing physical activity
  • Late-night use causes sleep disturbance
  • Excessive use can cause eyestrain and discomfort

Mental health

  • Cyber bullying can damage mental health
  • Constantly comparing your lifestyle with the curated content in your feed leads to anxiety and lower self-esteem
  • Social pressure to follow the latest trends can cause stress
  • Continually checking for updates because of FOMO affects mental wellbeing
  • Work-life balance is reduced, because the same platforms are used for professional and personal purposes
  • Intense information overload can cause cognitive issues
  • Feelings can't be expressed as meaningfully as in real life, which can lead to social isolation
  • Worry that employers may run online background checks causes discomfort and anxiety if something embarrassing is found on a profile

Ideological polarisation is when people strongly disagree and drift apart because of differing beliefs or opinions. Groups become more divided in their ideas, making it hard to find common ground. It can happen because:

  • Social networks allow groups (echo chambers) to be created for like-minded individuals
  • They can limit or exclude exposure to, and expression of, alternative viewpoints
  • Information and viewpoints are selective in content
  • Users' views can be manipulated by others
  • They can create groups of geographically remote but like-minded individuals

Stereotyping is having a fixed set of ideas about what a person or group is like, even though those ideas may be wrong. Social networking can lead to stereotyping because:

  • Individuals can be subject to intense scrutiny and surveillance
  • Comments can be seen by extremely large numbers of readers
  • Comments can be made that would never be made face to face
  • Intent is lost — readers see only the comment, not the context, so jokes may not look like jokes and sarcasm is lost
  • Individuals may conform to the stereotypes social media demands, to 'fit in' with peers

Cognitive issues are difficulties with mental processes such as thinking, memory, problem solving, attention and perception. They can affect a person's ability to understand, learn and process information; examples include memory loss, difficulty concentrating and problems with decision making. Social networking can contribute because:

  • Excessive screen time leads to cognitive fatigue, affecting concentration and attention span
  • Constant exposure to a high volume of data can overwhelm cognitive processing, making it hard to retain or prioritise information
  • Constant multitasking divides attention and reduces concentration on tasks that need focus
  • Negative experiences such as cyber bullying and harassment cause emotional stress and harm cognitive wellbeing
  • Curated, idealised content with no real context increases comparison between your own lifestyle and the lifestyles of others you see online, which can hurt self-esteem
  • Constant FOMO about social events and 'must try' experiences fuelled by social media can create anxiety and cognitive stress
  • Privacy concerns about the potential misuse of personal information add to cognitive stress and anxiety

The impact of IT 12.4

IT has changed how almost every sector works. For each one, learn the specific technologies and what they let people or organisations do.

Sports

  • Referee assistance — technology such as VAR (video assistant referee) in football helps referees make accurate decisions during games
  • Ticketing and access control — digital ticketing and efficient access control systems streamline fans' entry to sports venues
  • Wearable tech — athletes use wearable devices such as fitness trackers to monitor their physical condition and performance in training
  • Improved broadcasting — high-quality sports broadcasts and easy online streaming
  • Fan experience in stadiums — interactive displays and better connectivity improve the in-stadium experience
  • Virtual training and simulation — advanced simulation software helps with strategy planning and skill development
  • Data management — managing vast amounts of sports data, including player statistics and team records

Manufacturing

  • Robotics — more robots for precision, speed and handling hazardous tasks
  • 3D printing — a valuable tool for rapid prototyping and for producing complex parts
  • Real-time monitoring — tracking manufacturing processes in real time, for instant decisions and adjustments
  • Automation — automating manufacturing processes increases efficiency and reduces human error
  • AI in design — AI helps design more efficient and innovative products
  • Cybersecurity — stronger measures protect sensitive manufacturing data and intellectual property

Healthcare

  • Remote monitoring devices — devices worn daily that keep track of a patient's vital signs, for example for diabetes, and alert the user and emergency contacts when a reading is too low or too high
  • Artificial limbs — let people with disabilities or injuries use a limb again, or replace it completely, and live a fuller life
  • Nanotechnology — nanobots can deliver medicine (for example in cancer treatment) precisely, avoiding and reducing unnecessary treatment reaching healthy cells
  • AI chat support — helps patients navigate healthcare services and answers general queries
  • Patient portals — online platforms to book and reschedule appointments and to keep track of medical records and prescriptions

Education

  • Interactive whiteboards — make presentations and notes more visually appealing and interactive
  • E-books and digital libraries — soft copies of books (e-books) and audio books are easily available, making resources more accessible
  • Educational video games — let learning happen from another perspective, making it more interesting
  • Digital portfolios — let students upload tests and assignments and keep track of them
  • Virtual classrooms — let learning happen anytime and anywhere in the world using video conferencing

Banking

  • ATMs — instant cash withdrawals and deposits and basic transactions without a bank teller
  • Digital wallets — store payment information and make online and in-store payments easy
  • E-statements — reduce paper use and provide instant account updates
  • Online banking — manage accounts, transfer money and pay bills without visiting a bank
  • Improved customer service — online 24/7 helplines help resolve issues without waiting for the weekend to pass

E-business and finance

Online businesses are now commonplace, and buying goods and services online is often seen as easier than going to a physical store. Goods and services are often cheaper online because businesses save on shop space and reduced utility bills. The main advantage is how quickly the customer receives products. Online shopping has become even more popular with smart devices, because apps let you order anything online from your phone, wherever you are. That is convenient when, say, you are out shopping and can't find the product in store but can order it straight away from the online store.

Online shopping can be better for the environment, because fewer vehicles may be used when people aren't driving to shops. But it is often difficult to judge the quality of products online or to check their size, so many products end up being returned, which may increase the number of vehicles used.

News and media

IT lets people access news stories from around the world quickly, and has made them more aware of world events. It has also let people become amateur journalists, recording events as they happen and using social media to broadcast them to a wider audience.

This has both positive and negative effects. It lets people see what happened at certain events when the media may have chosen to censor them. Others argue that it can be harmful, saying the media sometimes censor events because they believe it could be harmful for people to be fully aware of what happened.

People can also use IT to set up news feeds that draw stories from many different media sources. This saves time, because they don't need to visit each source and find the stories they want to read. They set their news interests and the software searches for stories it thinks will interest them.

Family and home

IT is used to control many aspects of the home, such as lighting and heating, and people can even use these systems remotely. This can save money and be better for the environment. Rather than having the heating on all day, or on a timer that starts at a set time, the user can choose to start the heating remotely at the precise time they are about to travel home.

IT is also built into many everyday devices in the home, such as washing machines and refrigerators — for example, showing how long a washing machine has left to finish washing clothes. These devices can also be a source of entertainment: families can gather to play games and quizzes on them, or stream music, which helps them interact and spend quality time together.

Government and politics

IT, especially social media, has become more prominent in government and politics. Social media is often used to raise awareness of political causes and issues, and many online petitions are set up every day asking people to sign in support of issues they care about.

Used this way, IT helps a government see what issues people have, which may help it prioritise what it discusses. It gives people a voice and can force the government to discuss issues they believe need resolving.

IT has also let governments make their services accessible online. For example, people may be able to pay taxes online and apply for documents such as a passport or driving licence. This is more convenient for people, and can save the government money because it needs fewer staff than if people had to meet face to face for these services.

Monitoring and surveillance

The use of IT for monitoring and surveillance has become very prominent. Many police authorities around the world use it to monitor possible criminal activity and to locate criminals after a crime.

One example is combining facial recognition software with CCTV footage. A criminal will often try to flee an area or country if they think they will be caught, perhaps by train or plane, and train stations and airports have a lot of CCTV footage. This footage can be scanned with facial recognition software to see whether the criminal passed through a station or took a particular train. This saves a lot of time, because previously a person would have had to watch hours of footage to spot the criminal.

More people are also adding CCTV technology to their homes. Special systems can monitor the area around a house, and if motion is detected the user gets an alert on their mobile phone and can view footage of their home instantly to see what or who caused it. Some systems let the user speak through a speaker, which may scare off someone planning to burgle the house.

People have also started to use surveillance in their vehicles. Insurance companies use dashboard cameras that start recording the driver's journey when the car is switched on, so that footage can show who was responsible in an accident.

Some argue that these uses of IT are relatively unpoliced at present. Some people strongly object to an average person being able to record them walking down the street or past their home, believing it is an invasion of privacy and that greater laws and regulations are needed to govern this use.

Technology-enhanced learning 12.5

Technology-enhanced learning covers the ways computers and the internet are used to deliver teaching and let people learn. For each method, know what it is, its advantages and its disadvantages.

Computer-based training (CBT)

  • A learning method that uses computers to deliver educational content
  • Allows self-paced learning without a tutor, making it flexible and independent
  • Resources are a mixture of multimedia elements such as audio, video and text, which makes learning more effective
  • Training is usually delivered in small, manageable chunks, which supports step-by-step learning
  • Tests and assignments are included and are marked automatically, so progress can be tracked
  • Learners can revisit content as many times as they need
  • Content is delivered in a standardised way, giving all users a consistent learning experience
  • Learning can take place virtually, from anywhere in the world

Advantages

  • Users can learn at their own pace
  • Different media can be used to create an interesting set of resources
  • Staff can improve their skills and knowledge, which can lead to promotion or additional pay

Disadvantages

  • Learners may not have a tutor to answer their questions
  • Staff may not be motivated to follow the training because nobody is watching and driving them to do so
  • Learners need complete focus and good discipline to finish the course, and may lose motivation
  • It is not as engaging as in-person classroom learning, which can make it boring and reduce motivation

Online tutorials

  • Digital guides that teach viewers how to complete or achieve a specific goal or task
  • Can be delivered using different media: text, video tutorials, audio tutorials and so on
  • Users learn at their own pace and in their own time
  • Users can view a tutorial repeatedly, as often as they like
  • Can be accessed from anywhere with an internet connection, which is convenient and cost-effective
  • A diverse range of tutorials covers topics such as baking, cleaning and coding

Advantages

  • Students can reach teachers and tutors they otherwise couldn't, giving a greater range of expertise to draw on
  • Students can study as and when they wish
  • Students and teachers don't have to be online at the same time, so there are fewer time constraints
  • No need to travel, so there are no geographical constraints on where students study or take courses
  • Students can share their studies, ideas and learning with a group without being physically together or needing a teacher present
  • Tutorials can be recorded, accessed at any time and replayed to review work
  • Material can be studied in any order, so students choose what they study
  • Students get instant feedback from periodic pre-set tests or tasks

Disadvantages

  • A reliable internet connection and a suitable computing device are needed, so a digital divide has to be overcome
  • A tutorial may not be at the right level for an individual student, who may then be disadvantaged
  • Without a teacher, students may lose motivation or miss important material
  • Teacher feedback may not be fast enough to resolve problems with the studies or tasks

Networked courses

  • A form of online collaborative learning that connects learners with other learners and with their tutors
  • Designed to help learners build relationships with each other and with their tutors
  • Helps learning, because several different people can give feedback in the discussion
  • Mixes live sessions with self-paced learning activities
  • Classes take place in virtual environments, allowing real-time interaction between tutors and learners
  • Time scales are more limited, because learners tend to work at the same pace so they can exchange useful feedback and comments
  • Learners and tutors can join from anywhere in the world, giving global participation

Advantages

  • Learners work with other learners and build relationships they can take forward beyond the course
  • Learners both gain and give feedback, so they build new skills and knowledge and offer their own to others
  • Learners may be more motivated to complete tasks because other people's learning depends on them

Disadvantages

  • Some learners find it difficult to contribute because they aren't sure what feedback to give
  • Some learners may dominate every discussion, not leaving room for others to contribute, and tutors may find them difficult to manage
  • Learners may only give positive feedback because they don't feel comfortable criticising others' work
  • If some learners don't complete tasks or give feedback, it can limit the learning of others because time scales are tight

Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs)

  • Large-scale, free or affordable online courses that anyone on the internet can join
  • Cover a diverse range of topics and often give a certificate on completion
  • Offer flexible learning for professional skills development or personal interest
  • Include filmed lectures and other resources such as reading material
  • Many also provide interactive user forums
  • Progress is easy to track through assignments and tests that are marked automatically or reviewed by peers

Advantages

  • Can be organised anywhere and at any time, as long as there is connectivity
  • Can use global resources
  • Can be in any language, to reflect students' first languages
  • Can use any online tools available or known to students in the region
  • Can use peer review, with no exam pressure
  • Time zones and physical boundaries can be ignored
  • Can be organised quickly, as soon as students are informed
  • Learning can be in informal surroundings, so students are more relaxed
  • Reduces costs to the college
  • There are no other students to distract the learner
  • Cross-discipline learning is easier
  • Learning can continue beyond the formal time scale of the course, encouraging active learning beyond course requirements

Disadvantages

  • Students who are not digitally literate can't fully take part or make use of the course materials
  • User-generated course materials may devalue or undermine the validity of the course
  • Students who can't self-motivate or set their own goals can't fully take part
  • Language barriers and translations may exclude or disadvantage some participants
  • Very large amounts of content are difficult to navigate, set up and work through without guidance
  • Practical experience and training are limited, as are resources for practical work
  • Some students may not be able or prepared to devote the time and effort the course needs

Video conferencing

  • People communicate using computers, displaying a video stream over an internet connection
  • Allows real-time conversations
  • Sessions can be recorded for later viewing or reference
  • Many people can join from anywhere in the world
  • A user can share the content on their screen with the other people in the conference
  • Video quality depends on the conferencing software and the internet connection, so it is not consistent
  • Only a limited number of people can join, because lesson quality can drop with connectivity and other technical issues
  • The software supports both voice and text-based communication

Advantages

  • Learners can ask questions in real time, which may improve their learning and understanding
  • Learners can see and hear their educator, which may feel like a personal learning experience and increase motivation because it is more engaging
  • The educator can often share their screen with learners, which is useful for a demonstration

Disadvantages

  • Video conferencing software can need high broadband speeds that may not be available in some areas
  • Only a small number of learners can take part, limited by the software and the connection, which may leave some people waiting a long time for a place on a course

Impact on student achievement and motivation

This can be asked as an 8-mark question, for example on the impact of computer-based training. The same points, and more, can be used for the impact on student achievement, learner autonomy and teacher motivation.

Positive impacts

  • Training is more accessible, available when and where it suits the student, at any time
  • Small amounts of information delivered step by step prompt learners to respond to a lesson at intervals or take brief tests regularly
  • Regular, instant or quicker feedback on responses and overall progress increases engagement, achievement and retention of knowledge and skills
  • Learners can take courses at their own pace, without pressure to keep up with or wait for their peers
  • Sets learning prerequisites for moving on to the next lesson, section or topic
  • Progress can be tracked more accurately and individual targets set
  • Course information is easily available and can be retrieved later for reference

Negative impacts

  • Reduced human interaction with teachers or instructors makes discussion more difficult
  • Reduced human interaction with fellow learners means less group work, discussion and mutual assistance
  • Teachers or instructors may not always be available when students need help
  • Learners with low motivation will fall behind in their studies
  • Without class discussion, some students may be confused or not fully understand
  • Slow or poor internet connections, older devices or power fluctuations may not allow easy access to course materials

Impact on teachers' motivation

Positive impacts

  • Digital tools make teaching more creative and fun
  • Teachers can keep learning new things online
  • Teaching can be more flexible
  • Online classes can have more students
  • Fast feedback helps teachers improve
  • Teachers can work with others worldwide and share resources and learning methods

Negative impacts

  • Dealing with technology can be hard
  • Online teaching means extra preparation
  • Not seeing students in person can be tough
  • Keeping students interested online is challenging
  • Worrying about students' tech problems
  • Checking students' work online can be difficult
