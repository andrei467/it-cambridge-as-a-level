Cambridge AS & A Level IT · Chapter 5 eSecurity

eSecurity

Your personal data is valuable, and attackers have many ways to try to get hold of it. This chapter covers how personal data is targeted through phishing, smishing, vishing and pharming, and the malware — from viruses to ransomware — used to attack computer systems.

Personal data 5.1

Before you start
  • Do you know that personal data includes data such as your name, your address, your telephone number and your email address?
  • Do you have a basic understanding of what a cyber attack is, and what cyber security means?

Introduction

The world of IT can be fun and exciting, but it also comes with dangers, so it's important to be aware of them and know how to protect yourself.

There are people online with malicious intentions, and the more aware you are of what they could do, the better protected you can be. Building this awareness, and spending time keeping it up to date, helps make sure that your time online — and further afield, with technology generally — doesn't put you, your data or your identity at risk.

Sadly, many people relax their safety measures online because they feel comfortable at home and assume nothing bad will happen. This makes genuine, if boring, safety advice easy to ignore, even though it still matters.

Personal data

Personal data is any data that relates to you and your identity. This includes data such as:

  • Name
  • Address
  • Telephone number
  • Email address
  • Bank details
  • Medical records
  • Salary
  • Political opinions

You should be very careful about revealing any of your personal data. Revealing it to another person — especially online — exposes you to risks such as identity theft, fraud, bullying and blackmail. These problems can arise simply because revealing more about your thoughts and feelings to people lets them use that against you later. It's a sinister way to think about it, but the moment you reveal any personal data to someone, you are giving them the potential to use it against you or your identity. That doesn't mean you should never talk about yourself — just that, especially with people you don't know online, it pays to recognise a danger and know how to keep your identity secure.

Just as you've probably been taught to lock doors, not talk to strangers, and avoid unsafe areas in daily life, the same caution is worth applying online.

Some guidelines that can help you keep your personal data confidential:

  • Use strong passwords on any account that holds personal data. A strong password includes a random mix of characters, numbers and symbols, and isn't an easily recognisable word.
  • Encrypt (scramble) any data you store on your computer, so it can't be read without a decryption key.
  • Have a firewall active, to scan incoming and outgoing traffic from your computer system.
  • Regularly scan your computer with up-to-date anti-virus software, used to identify and remove viruses.
  • Make use of biometric devices (which measure a person's biological data, such as a thumbprint) built into technology.
  • Only visit and give data to websites that are a trusted source.

  • Check the URL attached to any link that requests your data, to see if it is genuine.
  • Be cautious about any pictures or opinions you post or send to people.

How is personal data collected?

There are several ways an unauthorised person can try to collect your data. These include:

  • Phishing
  • Smishing
  • Vishing
  • Pharming

Phishing

Phishing is when a person sends an email that looks legitimate to a user. The email contains a link to a website that also looks legitimate. The user is encouraged to click the link and input personal data into a form on the website — or the email itself could simply ask the user to reply with their personal data. The user is tricked into giving away personal data to a source they believe is legitimate, when both the email and the linked website actually come from a fake, unauthorised source.

The personal data collected this way can then be used by the unauthorised person to commit fraud, or to steal the person's identity. Phishing emails often try to pressure the user, for example by threatening that they must click the link and fix an issue, or something further will go wrong. The aim of a phishing attack is to steal the user's personal data.

A real-life example of phishing

PayPal has been the target of phishing emails on several occasions. Users receive an email that looks as though it came from PayPal, using PayPal's own branding. The email typically warns of unusual account activity, or asks the user to verify their account, and asks them to click a link to log in. Because the user is already on the lookout for a real PayPal email, they can easily be caught out and hand over their login details on the fake page.

Legitimate companies are protective of their professional reputation, and carefully check all communications for correct spelling and grammar. A link in a genuine email will normally contain the company's actual domain name, which you can check by hovering over the link (or by right-clicking and inspecting it) without clicking it. If the address the link actually points to doesn't contain the correct domain name — for example, if the sender is user@paypal.co rather than user@paypal.com — this is a sign the email isn't from a legitimate source.

How to recognise phishing

There are several guidelines to be aware of, to avoid being caught out by phishing:

  • Don't open an email that isn't from a sender or trusted source you recognise.
  • A legitimate company will never ask you for your personal data using email. Be immediately suspicious of any email that requests your personal data.
  • A legitimate company will normally address you by your name. Be suspicious of any email that addresses you as 'Dear Member' or 'Dear Customer'.
  • Be suspicious of any email with poor grammar or spelling mistakes — legitimate companies check this carefully.
  • A link in a genuine email from a legitimate company will normally contain the company's domain name. Hover over, or right-click and inspect, a link before clicking it to check its actual address.

Smishing

Smishing (or SMS phishing) is a variant of phishing that uses text messages to lure the user into providing their personal data. The user is sent a text message that either contains a link to a website — in the same way as phishing — or asks them to call a telephone number to resolve an urgent issue.

The same advice for avoiding phishing applies to smishing. The user should question any links sent from an unknown or suspicious number at any time. If a user believes a message may be legitimate, it's best to type the company's domain name into their browser directly, rather than follow the link in the message. Users should also block any numbers they believe are suspicious, to prevent further risk of smishing from that number.

Vishing

Vishing (or voice phishing) has the same aim as phishing: to obtain a user's personal details. The user receives a telephone call, which could be from an automated system or from a real person.

An automated voice could tell the user that an issue has occurred, such as suspicious activity on their bank account, and ask them to call another number, or simply to press a digit to be connected to another automated system that asks them to provide their bank account details to resolve the issue. Once obtained, these details can be used to commit a crime against the user.

The automated system could be replaced by a real person, who tries to convince the user that there has been an issue with their account, and that they need to provide log-in details or a PIN so it can be resolved. The precaution for vishing is the same as for a suspicious call of any kind: no company will ever call and ask you to provide account details or a PIN over the phone, and if in doubt, hang up and call the company back on a number you already know or can verify independently.

Pharming

Pharming is when an unauthorised user installs malicious code on a person's hard drive or server. The malicious code is designed to redirect a user to a fake website when they type the address of a legitimate one. The fake website is designed to look just like the legitimate one, so the user doesn't realise they have been redirected, and enters their personal data into the fake site believing it to be the real one. The unauthorised person will now have the user's personal data.

A common technique used in pharming is DNS cache poisoning. This exploits vulnerabilities in the domain name server (DNS) system, and diverts internet traffic that was meant for a legitimate server to a fake one instead. The unauthorised user needs to find a way to install the malicious code on the computer — often hidden in an email attachment or link, so that it downloads when the user opens the attachment or clicks the link. The aim of a pharming attack, like phishing, is to steal the user's personal data.

A real-life example of pharming

In 2007, a number of well-known companies — including PayPal, eBay, Barclays Bank and American Express — were targeted in a pharming attack. Over a three-day period, hackers managed to infect some users' PCs with a pharming code, redirecting them to fake, localised versions of each company's website when they tried to visit the genuine sites, to try to steal their personal data.

The original infected email was set up to look like a shocking news story, to encourage users to click a link to find out more — downloading the malicious code onto their computer in the process. It was a fairly sophisticated attack, needing legitimate-looking fake websites set up for a large number of companies, and it isn't known exactly how much the hackers were able to obtain as a result.

How to prevent pharming

All of the guidelines for avoiding phishing and smishing are also relevant for pharming. There are also some further precautions that can help guard against pharming attacks specifically:

  • Have a firewall installed and operational. A firewall monitors the traffic coming into and out of your computer, checks it against set criteria, and will flag and stop any traffic that doesn't meet that criteria — such as suspicious traffic from malicious code trying to enter your system.
  • Have an anti-virus program installed, designed to detect malicious code. You need to scan your computer regularly to check for any malicious code; it's advisable to set up an automatic scan on a daily basis, and to leave it switched on whenever your computer is on.
  • Be aware when using public Wi-Fi connections. A hacker could directly access your computer and install malicious code if you are connected to a public Wi-Fi network. It's advisable to use a VPN on public Wi-Fi — it shields your internet activity and personal data from a hacker, making it more difficult for them to gain access to your computer.

Smishing can also be used as a form of pharming: the user is sent a link that, when clicked, downloads malware onto their mobile device. It's advisable to have security software installed on mobile devices, too, and to scan them regularly to detect any issues.

Malware 5.2

Malware (malicious software) refers to any software designed to disrupt or damage a computer system, or sometimes a user.

Key word
malware
Software that is malicious.

There are various types of malware. These include:

  • virus
  • trojan
  • worm
  • spyware
  • adware
  • rootkit
  • malicious bots
  • ransomware

The consequences for an individual or a business depend on the type of malware. Some malware may just cause a minor irritation, such as slowing a computer down, but it could be much more serious, leading to identity theft, corruption of data, or blackmail (either to do with personally collected data, or to restore blocked access).

Virus

A virus is the most commonly known type of malware. It is the only type of malware that infects new files on a computer system: it attaches itself to a clean file, replicates, then lays dormant until the computer causes the code to be executed. If an infected file has already been replicated, it may begin to perform actions immediately, or lay dormant until the computer causes the code to be executed. Once part of the network, a virus can be especially dangerous if it infects files on a server that are accessed by many different computers.

There are several signs that your computer may have been infected with a virus. These include:

  • Slower system performance
  • Files multiplying or duplicating on their own
  • Files being deleted without your knowledge

The aim of a virus is to corrupt and disrupt data in a computer system. It is mainly a method of sabotage.

Minimising the risk of a virus

If a virus manages to infect a computer, it can be very difficult to eradicate. A robust anti-virus solution is needed to minimise the risk of a virus. You should install an anti-virus program and regularly scan your computer. It is best to set up a daily automatic scan at a time when you know your computer is likely to be switched on.

Anti-virus software scans a computer system and finds any files it thinks contain a virus. It will quarantine these files and alert the user of their presence. The user can then select to delete these files. It is also possible to remove this stage and set the software to automatically delete all quarantined files.

One weakness of anti-virus software is that it is dependent on the database it holds. If a perpetrator manages to hack a system with a virus that isn't in the database, it won't be recognised and removed, which could leave it to do a great deal of damage. For this reason, it is also important to update your anti-virus software to make sure it includes the latest known viruses.

Some anti-virus software can be set for real-time checking. This means that all programs that are requested for download are immediately checked for viruses. If the anti-virus detects the presence of a virus, it will alert the user and tell them not to download the file as it contains a virus.

You should also be very careful when sharing any resources using portable storage devices such as USB memory sticks. You should immediately scan any USB memory stick that is inserted into your computer, even if it is your own storage device. It is very common for viruses to spread through the use of portable storage devices.

Trojan

A trojan is a type of malware that disguises itself as legitimate software, or is included within legitimate software that has been tampered with. The term "trojan" comes from Greek mythology: the Greeks gave a wooden horse as a peace offering to the people of the city of Troy. The horse actually contained several soldiers, who climbed out at night and opened the city gates to the rest of the army, who were then able to conquer the city.

A malware trojan works in a similar way. It looks harmless, but hides a malicious program. When the trojan file is opened, it will normally release another type of malware, such as a virus. A trojan needs the user to run the program for it to release other malicious software, so it will usually encourage the user to run the program, for example by telling the user that another program needs to be updated, and to click a program to update it. This then runs the trojan, and allows it to release further damage.

Minimising the risk of a trojan

It is difficult to minimise the risk of trojans, because they mask themselves as legitimate software. They also require the user to run them, so rely on the error of a user operating, rather than a flaw in the software or a firewall. It is more likely that a user would make an error and be tricked into running the software than that a weakness in a firewall or anti-virus would let the malware go undetected.

The main way to minimise the risk of a trojan is to only open files and click to run software that is from a trusted source, for example trusted companies and trusted websites. If you are in any doubt about the program, do not run it.

Worm

A worm is a type of malware that acts in a similar way to a virus, but with a fundamental difference. A worm is a program that replicates itself, like a virus, but unlike a virus it does not need to attach itself to another program or file to cause damage. Worms exploit security holes and issues in a computer. These normally exist in the operating system.

A worm replicates itself and aims to fill up all of the free space on a computer to slow it down and bring it to a halt. Therefore, the first signs for a user that their computer has been infected with a worm is that it starts to run slowly and the space on their hard drive begins to rapidly decrease.

A worm also tries to spread to different computers on a network. For this reason, worms are often used to infect a large number of computers on a network. If a worm is able to spread throughout a network, it can clog up bandwidth and slow the whole network down.

Worms are normally downloaded and spread through email attachments, peer-to-peer file-sharing networks, or using a link to a website or resource. Once downloaded, they do not need any other human interaction to replicate themselves.

A well-known example of a computer worm is Stuxnet. Stuxnet was a computer worm that was used as a cyber weapon in 2010. It was discovered by two security researchers who recognised that the worm was far more complex than any they had seen at the time. The worm was being used to attack a power plant, and security experts believed the aim was to sabotage nuclear weapon production.

Minimising the risk of a worm

Worms often aim to exploit software vulnerabilities in a computer. These will normally be located in the operating system or applications. Therefore, you should regularly check for and install updates for your operating system and applications. If this process can be set to occur automatically, this removes the need to remember to do it.

The same guidelines about minimising phishing should also be followed, as this can help reduce the risk of downloading a worm attached to an email attachment or a link.

Anti-virus software can normally check for worms too. Therefore, regularly scanning your computer with anti-virus software can help identify a worm that has infected your computer.

Worms can be spread through network connections. Therefore, disconnecting your computer from a network, when the network resources are not required, can keep it safe during this time.

Spyware

Spyware is a general term used to describe malware that is designed to gather information about your interactions with your computer. As the name suggests, the aim of spyware is to spy on the user.

A common example of spyware is a key logger. A key logger is installed on a user's computer, normally without their consent. The key logger will then record any key presses carried out by the user. All of this data is sent to a third party, normally the person who created the spyware, to be analysed. The data is analysed, normally by another computer, to establish any patterns in the data. The patterns are then analysed to see if any of them look as though they could be personal or sensitive data, for example a password.

Spyware can perform actions beyond just key logging. A user can sometimes unknowingly allow a commercial company to use spyware for several purposes, including:

  • Targeted marketing from tracking browsing habits
  • Sending unwanted and often irritating pop-up adverts
  • Installing add-ons and redirecting to advertising websites

You can often unknowingly download spyware while using the internet. It can be embedded into enticing opportunities, such as a pop-up advert offering a prize or a free product. It can also be embedded into the download of a video, music or application file, those that are free of cost. Sometimes this may involve consenting to the download of the spyware, and can go unread and unnoticed if this consent is buried in the small print.

Cookies are a type of spyware that you may consent to, used to track your internet surfing habits. Even with cookies, you should always check what you are allowing the company to do with your data shared with third parties.

Minimising the risk of spyware

Spyware is always roaming around the internet, so it is important to understand how you can minimise the risk of allowing it to be used to monitor your actions.

As always, be very careful about what you download, especially when downloading software that is free of cost, and downloading from video, music and file-sharing sites. Make sure that this is only done from trustworthy and reputable sources.

Do not click on any links or offers in pop-up adverts, no matter how enticing they may seem. If there is no obvious way to close a pop-up window, you can press ALT and F4, and this will close your internet session.

Always read the small print when consenting to any user agreement. Commercial companies can sometimes list adware in their product by claiming that it will generate revenue for them, keeping the cost of the product lower.

Key word
anti-malware
Software that is used to identify malware on a computer and remove it.

Anti-malware software can be used to scan your computer to see if any key-logging software is present. This type of software will normally remove any key logger it finds. If it does find a key logger, it is advisable to change all your passwords immediately, in case any of your passwords have been gathered and analysed, before the issue occurs in the future.

Adware

Adware is a type of software designed to display targeted advertising on your computer. It does this by collecting data about your internet browsing habits. Adware can be legitimate, or it can be illegitimate.

Legitimate program developers will justify the inclusion of adware in their product by claiming that it will generate revenue for them, keeping the cost of the product lower. Adware can be bundled with legitimate software downloads. This means you may end up downloading adware onto your computer without actually asking for it, and this can happen when you are given the chance to customise what is downloaded. For example, there may be a hidden addition to the download of a task or search bar added into your current internet browser. You can customise the download and uncheck the box so that you do not get the adware, but if you do not know to do this you may download it unknowingly.

Once downloaded, adware can prove difficult to delete, as they do not normally have an uninstall feature. It may not act maliciously, but it will often serve as a method of advertising for the company, or try to get you to use their search function.

Adware as malware will present adverts when a user is browsing the web, that are often shown constantly. They are normally in the form of pop-ups or windows that cannot be closed. They can often be very irritating.

Minimising the risk of adware

You may be happy to allow some adware to track your browsing habits online. This will allow you to see adverts for products that you might be interested in. You may not be happy to allow adware to make your browsing experience become constantly interrupted by having too many adverts popping up on a constant basis. Therefore, you may want to be careful about what adware you allow to be installed on your computer.

The most proactive measure you can take with adware is to make sure that you check exactly what is downloaded onto your computer. Look at the list of component parts that will be downloaded, and make sure that any that look like they could be adware (for example, a search bar or task bar addition to your browser) are not ticked. If they are present, untick them immediately before allowing the download to go ahead.

Once downloaded, unwanted adware can be very difficult to remove. It may take several scans with anti-malware software to detect and remove the adware.

Rootkit

A rootkit is a computer program that enables a person to gain administrator access to a victim's computer. They are designed to stay hidden on a user's computer, and allow the computer to be controlled from a remote location. A rootkit allows the unauthorised user to carry out several illegal tasks with the computer, use the computer as part of a larger cyber attack, or to steal personal data and information.

Rootkits can get installed because a victim's password is cracked, or a vulnerability in the security of a computer system is exploited. The person installing it can then use the access to stop the computer recognising that the rootkit is there, so the victim will not know that someone else has complete access to their system. The rootkit will normally be buried deep within the operating system, so that it can try to avoid any detection by anti-malware software. Other malware can be incorporated into a rootkit, which can then be concealed on the computer to cause harm.

One very sneaky way that an unauthorised person can use to install a rootkit on a computer is by leaving USB memory sticks, infected with a rootkit, in places they believe will be found. They are relying on the curiosity of another user to insert the USB memory stick that they have found into their computer, and therefore download the rootkit.

An early rootkit example is NTRootkit. This appeared in 1999, and was designed to infiltrate Windows operating systems. A rootkit designed to do the same with Mac operating systems didn't appear until 10 years later.

Minimising the risk of a rootkit

Rootkits are often installed through your computer password being cracked, or through another method such as being embedded in a software download or installed on a USB memory stick.

It is advisable to have a strong password set for your computer, to minimise the risk of it being cracked. A strong password should contain a random mixture of characters, numbers and symbols. It should not contain any data that could be connected to you or your likes and dislikes. It is also advisable to change your password on a regular basis, in case it has been discovered.

Previous advice should also be followed about making sure that software is only downloaded from trusted and reputable sources. You should also, under no circumstances, insert a USB memory stick that you find into your computer. It may be tempting to see what it contains, but the risks that could be attached to inserting it into your computer are not worth the temptation.

Removing a rootkit from a computer once it has been installed can be extremely difficult. It is software that is designed to be hidden and heavily relies on this feature. A rootkit may be detected by anti-malware, but this is highly unlikely. Often, one of the only ways to rid a computer of a rootkit is to completely reinstall the operating system.

Malicious bots

A bot, short for robot, is an application used to carry out simple, repetitive tasks that are automated. These are normally tasks that would be mundane and time-consuming for a human to find and do. A bot could be used for very productive reasons, such as indexing a search engine, but they can also be used as a type of malware.

Malicious bots are used by cybercriminals in a variety of ways:

  • SPAM bots are used to bombard people's email inboxes with SPAM emails.
  • Zombie bots are used to create a bot network. The bot will lay dormant on a computer until an attack is launched. The computer will then be connected with lots of other computers that have been compromised to launch a large-scale attack on an organisation. Computers connected this way can be known as a botnet.
  • Chatter bots will pretend to be humans or sites, such as social networking and dating sites. They will try to emulate human interaction, with the goal of obtaining personal data.
  • File-sharing bots will provide a search result for a user's search request, and offer a link for file download.

Bots often have worm-like capabilities, because they can replicate and spread themselves.

Minimising the risk of bots

As bots can often be embedded into links or software downloads, and are often spread in the same way that phishing is carried out, the same measures that can be applied to minimise phishing can also help minimise the risk from bots. The best advice is to think carefully about who you are talking to and whether they link you will click on any links without first checking who sent them and that the link will take you to a trusted resource.

As bots can often be used in a chat situation, you should never give out any personal data when chatting online. Even if you feel this is in a private environment, one with another person, you should still never provide any personal data. If you are chatting to friends, you should agree to never request any personal data from each other online. If you are chatting to what you believe is a friend, if you feel they ask you an odd question, or for personal data, then contact them by another means, such as calling them, and double check that you are speaking to your friend.

If you suspect that you have downloaded a bot, anti-malware software can be used to detect and remove it.

A firewall can also be used to detect the activity of a bot, as it may recognise suspicious traffic created by the bot. However, if a bot is sophisticated and can disguise its communications as legitimate traffic, it will go unnoticed by a firewall.

Ransomware

Ransomware is a type of malware that restricts a user's access to their computer system and files. The ransomware will normally demand that the user pays a ransom in order to regain access to their computer system. Some ransomware programs will completely lock a user's system, and some will encrypt all of the files on their system in a way that renders them useless. Ransomware will normally try to enter a system in a similar way to a trojan. If the user doesn't pay the ransom in a set amount of time, they risk losing their data forever.

A well-known example of ransomware is WannaCry, a 2017 attack that spread rapidly across the world. The unauthorised user demanded that an infected user pay a $300 ransom. If the user did not pay within three days, the amount was doubled. If payment was not made within a week, the unauthorised user threatened to delete the infected user's files forever.

The success rate of a person paying the ransom relies on the user's attachment to the data stored on their computer. Users who do pay the ransom can often be exploited further, and asked for a further payment to release their data, since paying does not guarantee the data will actually be returned.

Minimising the risk of ransomware

The main way to minimise the risk of ransomware is to make sure you have a copy of your data. If you create a separate backup of your data, and keep it up to date, then the risk of losing your data in a ransomware attack becomes greatly reduced. This means that you can reinstate your data if it is deleted or locked.

The risk of losing data can also be reduced by storing data in the cloud. Cloud systems often have a facility that allows the user to roll back to a previous version of the data, so it can be reinstated if lost.

Because ransomware often enters a system in a similar way to a trojan or through phishing, the same general precautions — only running trusted software, and being cautious of suspicious links and attachments — also help reduce the risk of a ransomware infection in the first place.
